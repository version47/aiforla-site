Our top 5 takeaways

01

Working curricula are organized by business function, not AI concept

The strongest programs teach marketing, customer service, operations, and finance with AI inside each, not "Intro to AI." Small Business B(AI)sics, Bronx's five buckets, America's SBDC AI-U, and Florida SBDC all use this spine. Owners buy outcomes for jobs they recognize.

02

The programs that change businesses end every session with a working tool

Brazoria's rule is "Ship every Saturday": every session ends with an automation running in the owner's real business, on their own data. Florida SBDC runs on "build, test, apply in your own operations." Lecture-and-framework formats send owners home with a mental model and homework.

03

The best on-ramps relate to the owners from a non-technical perspective first.

The best on-ramps name the owner's situation, not the technology. FLAIA's event is called "AI for Confused Business Owners." Brazoria promises "if you can use Gmail, you have everything you need on Day 1." Bronx spends week one defusing "AI is coming for my job." All three are examples incorporating fear reduction into the fabric of the curriculum.

04

Events-only groups without a progression stall small

FLAIA draws 1 to 3 people per event with ~81 members. Applied AI Tampa held one event and sits at 28 members. The durable programs run a ladder: a low-friction social entry, a short finishable cohort, then a contributor tier like the SBDC's student-staffed AI Clinics.

05

Success rides on many partners

Every solo-organizer group in the data stalled, even the ones with Google as the sponsor. The durable structures, America's SBDC network and Southern Georgia Black Chambers, sit on multiple funders and multiple institutional partners.

Cross-cutting lenses

What the field is telling us

Patterns that hold across the 19 profiles, read through three lenses. Curriculum gets the most room because it's where the field separates.

The priority lens

Curriculum & pedagogy

The programs that actually move non-technical owners share one pedagogical DNA: curriculum organized by business function, hands-on sessions grounded in the owner's own business and data, and a deployed artifact at the end. The biggest single design decision in the data is the shape of the entry level.

Patterns across the field
  • Function-organized: marketing, customer service, operations, finance, prefaced by AI basics and data safety (B(AI)sics, Bronx, SBDC AI-U, Florida SBDC).
  • Ship in the room, on the owner's own data, with no deferred homework (Brazoria, Florida SBDC).
  • Zero-background promises stated plainly: "if you can use Gmail and a spreadsheet" (Brazoria).
  • A day-one business-context exercise grounds every later prompt (Anthropic); Welcome to Chinatown maps the workflow before anyone opens a laptop.
  • Tracks end with artifacts and a credential: a workflow template, a written AI-use policy, a certificate (Anthropic, B(AI)sics).
  • Short, predictable time commitments: 1 hr/week for 6 weeks (Manhattan), 4 x 2 hrs (Florida SBDC), 4 Saturdays (Brazoria).
Strongest examples
  • Brazoria Tech Lab · A 4-weekend arc (triage tools, plan workflows, build an assistant, deploy lead gen) where every Saturday ends with a working automation in the owner's real business.
  • AI Fluency (Anthropic) · A named framework (4D), a day-one business-context doc, and learners leave with a workflow template and a written AI-use policy.
  • Bronx AI Advantage · Five function buckets across four weeks, with tracked outcomes: 5-10 hrs/week saved, ~$500/week in cost savings.
What stalls in the data
  • Lecture-plus-framework entry levels: UGA's workshop is explicitly not hands-on building; owners leave with a mental model and homework.
  • Events with no curriculum at all: FLAIA, Charlotte, Memphis, Tampa, and Main Street have hooks and formats but no leveled path and no artifact.
  • Re-attendance mistaken for progression: at Memphis, depth accrues through coming back weekly; there are no defined levels.
Lens 02

Events & community ops

Two operating models cluster: low-friction recurring touchpoints (coffee chats, biergarten Q&As) that meet owners where they are emotionally, and finite high-commitment cohorts that change how a business runs. The strongest operators run the cohort, and several pay owners on completion rather than charging them.

Patterns across the field
  • Third-place entries: 30-minute coffee chats (Memphis), a biergarten Q&A (FLAIA), food-and-drinks storytelling nights with ~50 attendees (Welcome to Chinatown).
  • Finite cohorts of 15-40 seats, 4 to 6 weeks, weekly sessions (Bronx, Manhattan, Brazoria).
  • Completion-conditioned money: Manhattan's $5,000 for implementing, Bronx's $350K grant pool, Southern Georgia's $2,500 Master Grant.
  • Oversubscription: Bronx drew 200+ applicants for 70 seats.
  • Accessibility mechanics: Spanish-language sessions (Wisconsin; Southern Georgia's Project Puente), after-hours and weekend slots (Alabama), rotating neighborhood venues (Memphis).
Strongest examples
  • Bronx AI Advantage · The strongest end-to-end operator: a tight 4-week cohort taught by local founder-practitioners, hard tracked outcomes, and $350K in completion grants.
  • Manhattan Tech to Table · One vertical, one hour a week for six weeks, and a $5,000 grant conditioned on implementation, not attendance.
  • Welcome to Chinatown · Owner-to-owner demos over food and drinks work as teaching and recruiting at once.
What stalls in the data
  • A strong hook with no ladder: FLAIA (~81 members, 1-3 per event), Applied AI Tampa (one event, 28 members), Main Street (drop-in only).
  • Solo-organizer dependency: Tampa's group went quiet after a single event with a single organizer.
  • A paywall before trust: Tampa and Charlotte charged for beginner content before building an audience.
Lens 03

Entity & funding models

Five organizational forms show up, on a clear durability spectrum. Institutional networks with distribution rails and multiple funders are the most robust; solo-organizer meetups are the most fragile. Nearly every high-reach program rides a single large corporate grant.

Patterns across the field
  • Institutional networks: America's SBDC AI-U ($10M Google.org on top of permanent SBA, university, and state funding; 63 networks, ~1,000 centers) and the U.S. Chamber's B(AI)sics ($5M Google.org through 1,500+ chambers).
  • Chamber foundations running grant-funded cohorts: Bronx and Manhattan, both on Google.org money.
  • Diversified local coalitions: Southern Georgia Black Chambers stacks HUD dollars, a Georgia Power sponsorship, VSU, UGA SBDC, and a Latino association.
  • For-profit funnels: Memphis's meetup feeds the WeAddBots consultancy, Julia Fu's masterclasses feed a paid institute, Brazoria charges tuition with sponsor seat bundles.
  • Solo-organizer groups with no entity and no funding: FLAIA, Tampa, Charlotte.
Strongest examples
  • America's SBDC AI-U · The host network is permanently co-funded by the SBA, universities, and states, so the $10M grant is accelerant rather than lifeblood; student clinics form a renewable contributor pipeline.
  • Southern Georgia Black Chambers · Five-plus funders and partners; no single one can sink it.
  • Wisconsin SBDC + SWWRPC · A $68K USDA grant covered a five-county free series, reported in econ-dev metric language: 9 jobs created, 9 saved.
What stalls in the data
  • Single-funder dependency across the high-reach set: most of the biggest programs ride one Google.org grant.
  • Single-organizer dependency: FLAIA at 1-3 attendees, Tampa after one event.
  • Vendor capture: where a consultancy funds the room, trust holds only when selling is walled out (Memphis's no-pitch rule).
